What was tested

750 synthetic pools (lt001–lt750) were registered against the leios network and their BLS keys merged into 76-key arrays on the ten leiosred*-bp-* block producers. The chain sits at 900 live pools, exactly the CIP-0164 committee cap. The synthetic seats hold negligible stake, so they add vote volume without adding vote weight.

Keys were deployed at ~14:02. The three leios[1-3]-bp-* hosts kept a single real key each and are the control group, the most useful instrument in the test.

Only those three have a topology fixed by configuration rather than by chance: cardano-parts sets useLedgerAfterSlot = -1 and bootstrapPeers = null for the bp role and takes producers from the filtered intra-cluster list as trustable localRoots, so no ledger peers, no peer sharing, and 4 hot peers as a configured constant rather than an emergent value. The ten leiosred*-bp-* hosts are not like this — leiosRedTeamBp imports leiosBp but then forces services.cardano-node-topology.role to edge — so they use ledger peers and peer sharing, and their connection counts drift as the relays' do.

The controls are not restart-free either. Every leios host carries RuntimeMaxSec = 12 * 3600 (flake/colmena.nix) to cap a node memory leak before it reaches OOM, so all 19 hosts restart on a staggered 12-hour timer throughout. See Methodology for what that does and does not explain.

The underlying transaction load

Two distinct loads are in play and it matters which one is which. The vote load is what this test changed — 750 extra committee seats casting votes. The transaction load is the pre-existing background that drives block and EB production, and it was not raised for this test. It is described here because it sets the duty cycle that every comparison in this document is phase-aligned to.

Transactions come from tx-centrifuge on leios1-centrifuge-a-1, configured to submit to one relay in each group — leios1-rel-a-1, leios2-rel-b-1 and leios3-rel-c-1 on port 3001, the node-to-node port. Its rate_limit.params.tps is 100, but that is a ceiling rather than a target: the achieved rate ran 32.7–46.4 tx/s across the nine 10-09 windows.

A second generator runs alongside it: tx-firehose on leios2-rel-b-2 (the leiosFirehose import in flake/colmena.nix), on the same even-hour cadence — cardano-tx-firehose.timer is also OnCalendar=00/2:00:00, with its own stop timer and the same 4200s backstop. Both generators are therefore submitting during exactly the same hours.

The two are cleanly separable on chain by their transaction metadata, and db-sync over the 58-hour window gives the split:

generator tx size metadata on chain count by count by bytes
tx-centrifuge 506 bytes key 0, 256-byte payload 267 bytes 3,335,846 73.9% 85.3%
tx-firehose 247 bytes key 1022, colour tag 0x532bee 8 bytes 1,176,195 26.1% 14.7%

Centrifuge's 256-byte payload reaches 267 bytes on chain once CBOR framing is counted. The firehose tags each transaction with a 3-byte run colour instead, which is why its transactions are less than half the size.

Because the two sizes differ by more than 2×, count and bytes tell different stories: the firehose is a quarter of the transactions but under a sixth of the bytes, 290.5 MB against centrifuge's 1,687.9 MB over the 58 hours. Which denominator is the right one depends on the question, and anything downstream of EB size wants bytes. A single mean transaction size across both — 438 bytes — describes neither generator.

Both shapes appear in off-hours too, at roughly 7% of on-hour volume, which is the mempool draining rather than a third source. Separately, 1,055 transactions in the window carry no metadata at all, 1,020 of them a uniform 202 bytes confined to 16:54–20:05 on 10-09; they are 0.023% of the total and are not attributed here.

The duty cycle is one hour on, one hour off, driven by a pair of systemd timers: cardano-tx-centrifuge.timer fires OnCalendar=00/2:00:00, so the generator starts on even hours at :00, and a separate stop timer halts it on odd hours. RuntimeMaxSec=4200 on the service is a backstop should the stop timer not fire, not the mechanism.

Averaged over the whole weekend, fleet relay egress by minute of the two-hour cycle shows the shape clearly:

phase minutes into cycle fleet egress
generator ramp 0–4 90.2 Mbit/s
steady on 5–59 103.8
post-stop decay 60–64 58.2
off 65–119 16.5

Steady-on carries 6.3× the off-hour level. Two consequences follow. First, an on-hour and an off-hour are not comparable at all, so every before/after offset in this document is an even number of hours. Second, the generator does not stop cleanly: traffic takes about four minutes to drain after the stop timer fires, as in-flight transactions, EBs and votes finish propagating.

The analysis windows are anchored at :05 rather than :00, so each one skips the ramp and instead includes those four minutes of decay at its end — roughly 55 minutes of steady load plus a short tail. Every window in the test uses the same convention, loaded and unloaded alike, so comparisons between them remain like-for-like.

Note that the transaction rate was lower during the loaded windows (32.7–39.7 tx/s) than during the unloaded baselines (42.3–46.4 tx/s). It is tracked as a confounder in the tables below: any measure that rises under load does so despite slightly less transaction traffic, not because of more.

Node build: Leios prototype w40a. The nine relays run the governor-patched variant (node-leios-patched); every other host runs plain node-leios. Every number in this document is specific to that build. w40a carries a known network incompatibility with earlier prototypes (leiosNotifyPipelineDepth raised 100 → 1000), so results here should not be compared against measurements taken on w39 or earlier. Nothing here distinguishes behaviour specific to w40a from behaviour intrinsic to Leios, so treat every finding as scoped to this build until it is reproduced on another.

Headline

The load ran continuously from 2026-10-09 14:02 through 2026-10-12 00:00, covering 29 loaded on-hours. How much "vote volume" rose depends on what is counted, and the four answers differ by an order of magnitude:

measure unloaded loaded ratio
committee seats, from the maximum voterId observed ~142 900 6.3×
seats actually casting votes 90 788+ 8.8×
vote records diffusing, per node 1.91/s 28.63/s 15×
our own fleet's cast rate 24.5/min 1654/min 67×

The headline figure for the network is 15×, the rate at which each node actually has to receive and process votes. The 67× is our fleet's own casting rate, large only because our block producers went from 13 voting keys to 763 — three control hosts at one key each plus ten leiosred hosts at 76 — moving us from roughly 14% of all active voters to the large majority of them. It is a statement about our share of the committee, not about network load.

Those key counts are the physical ones. Mimir's count by (instance, voterId) reads 15 and 765 because leiosred2-bp-b-1 and leiosred6-bp-f-1 each carry series for both voterId 12 and 13, with only one incrementing on each host: two stale zero-rate series, cross-contaminated between the pair. Series counts include series that have stopped incrementing, so they are not a key count.

Three measured costs:

  • Node CPU, relay mean in cores: unloaded 0.313–0.400, loaded 0.50–0.64 across sampled weekend on-hours. Every sampled hour clears the unloaded band.
  • EB announcement late fraction: unloaded 0.00–1.33%, loaded 2.8–6.1%, sustained for 58 hours.
  • Certification rate is down. Certified EBs per forged EB: 32.5% loaded (230 of 707) against 38.1% unloaded (261 of 685), z ≈ 2.17, p ≈ 0.030.

Relay bandwidth shows no vote-attributable effect. Comparing each statistic against its own kind across the 29 loaded on-hours: hourly means run 73.9–136.5 Mbit/s against unloaded means of 86.8 and 118.0, and 1-second fleet peaks run 1304–2048 Mbit/s against unloaded peaks of 1776 and 2278. The largest burst anywhere in the test, 2278 Mbit/s, falls in an unloaded hour — despite the loaded set having 29 hours in which to beat it against the baseline's 2.

Mean vote tally cannot be used to corroborate anything here: its denominator is vote arrivals, which rose ~15×, so the metric moves mechanically under this load.

Relay egress tracks peer degree and block serving rather than vote volume: the relays that swing hardest between any two windows are the ones whose duplex connection counts moved, and relay connection counts vary by up to 40% between windows. That link is inferred from correlation on the relays, which do use peer sharing and ledger peers.

The control BPs are the experiment that does hold topology fixed while vote load varies: their peer set is pinned by configuration at 4 hot peers, and their egress stayed flat across every window.

What this changes

The traffic is extremely bursty. Median per-relay egress is 2–32 Mbit/s while 1-second peaks reach 585 Mbit/s. Fleet peak-to-mean ratios run 11–23×. The link is near-idle most of the time and spikes roughly 100× for brief moments.

Means are unaffected; only peaks are. A mean is independent of the averaging window, since both instruments are computing bytes-per-hour over the same hour. Mimir's mean of 5-minute averages and netdata's mean of 1-second samples agree to within 0.4–2.9% on all six 10-09 windows. So every "mean" in this document is a 1-hour mean whichever tool produced it, and the discrepancy below applies to peaks alone.

The 5-minute metric understates peaks by 15–25×. Mimir reported leios1-rel-a-1's load1 peak egress as 14.0 Mbit/s. The true 1-second peak that hour was 237 Mbit/s. Fleet-wide, momentary egress reaches 1.0–2.3 Gbit/s against a 5-minute-averaged fleet total of 80–120 Mbit/s.

Every "sustained" figure in this document therefore describes a 5-minute average and says nothing about instantaneous load. Peaks are an order of magnitude higher, and 585 Mbit/s on a single relay is over half a 1 Gbps link.

The load conclusion holds at this resolution and is strengthened by it:

  • The largest single-relay peak anywhere in the dataset, 585 Mbit/s, is in base_11 — an unloaded hour.
  • Fleet peak egress is highest in an unloaded hour, 2278 Mbit/s against 1776 for the other baseline hour. Across all 29 loaded on-hours through 10-12 the peak range is 1304–2048, so none reaches the unloaded maximum, even with 29 chances against 2.
  • Per relay, taking the maximum across all 29 loaded hours against the maximum across the two unloaded hours, 7 of 9 relays stay below their unloaded peak. Two exceed it: leios1-rel-a-2 at 492 against 383 Mbit/s, and leios3-rel-c-3 at 469 against 462.

That last point needs care rather than rounding off. The comparison is the maximum of 29 draws against the maximum of 2, so the loaded side has far more chances to produce a high value even if the underlying distributions are identical. Two relays edging past by 2% and 28% is what that asymmetry alone would produce; it is not evidence of a load effect, but neither can the per-relay comparison be stated as a clean sweep.

The fleet-level comparison does not suffer the same problem as badly, because fleet peaks aggregate nine relays per sample, and there none of the 29 loaded hours reaches the unloaded maximum. Measuring at the resolution where bursts actually live, the unloaded hours still bound the loaded ones.

Headroom, honestly stated. Sustained utilisation is trivial, but peaks of 300–585 Mbit/s per relay are a real fraction of a gigabit link, and that was true before the vote load. node_network_*_drop_total is a counter rather than an average, so it catches loss at any timescale, and it is zero across every window. Nothing is being dropped — but the headroom is smaller than the averaged metrics imply.

They differ from the loaded BPs in two respects at once, not one: 1 BLS key instead of 76, and 4 hot peers instead of 20. So the level difference between the groups — control BPs under 28 Mbit/s, loaded BPs reaching 41–300 — cannot be attributed to key count from this comparison alone; peer count is a sufficient alternative explanation and the two are confounded between groups.

What the control group does establish is the within-group result, and that is the one that matters. Their peer set is pinned by configuration, so it is constant across unloaded and loaded hours while vote load varies by the full amount. Egress does not move. That is a controlled before/after on the only variable that changed for them, and it is why these three hosts carry more weight than any relay comparison in this document.

Certification — reduced under load

The tally metric cannot be used here

Mean vote tally is not a valid before/after comparator under this load. Tally is a stake-weight fraction in 0–1, compared against a quorum threshold of 0.75. The tally histogram records one sample per vote arrival, and tally updates rose ~15× with the load. Each update samples the accumulation curve toward quorum, so denser sampling of the low part of that curve drags the mean down mechanically. Any reading of mean tally across this step, in either direction, is an artifact of the denominator.

The tally panel remains a valid live view of distance to quorum. Only the before/after comparison is invalid.

What can and cannot be said

Can be said: the certified fraction is 5.6 points lower under load, the effect clears p < 0.05 on 707 loaded and 685 unloaded forged EBs. Individual loaded and unloaded blocks overlap.

Cannot be said: which mechanism. Loaded blocks span 26.5–38.3% and unloaded 34.0–42.3%, so the distributions overlap; the separation is in the pooled means, not in every block.

Possible explanations, none of which this data distinguishes:

  1. Diffusion strain — real weighted votes arriving too late to be tallied before the EB's window closes. The elevated EB announcement late fraction is consistent with this.
  2. Committee composition — certification needs a stake-weight fraction of 0.75, and the synthetic pools hold negligible stake, so on the face of it they add seats without removing weight and should not move the ratio at all. That reasoning holds only if every real-stake pool keeps its seat. The chain now sits at exactly the 900-pool CIP-0164 cap, leaving no headroom: if seat allocation samples or rotates rather than seating all 900 each epoch, a real pool that loses its seat takes its stake weight out of the tally with it, and 0.75 becomes harder to reach. Whether allocation behaves that way is not established by this test — it is a question about the selection rule, not about capacity.

A post-load recovery period showing the ratio return to the 34–42% band would confirm the load is the cause rather than a drift over the week. That is the cheapest remaining test.

Chain-level health was otherwise normal throughout: density 4.3→4.8% of slots carrying a block, blockNum spread of 2 blocks across 26 hosts, hot peers pinned at 20, no ServerError, no fork or island.

Is it the w40 / w40a incompatibility?

w40a raises leiosNotifyPipelineDepth from 100 to 1000. Both sides read their own copy of the constant, so a w40a initiator pipelines up to 1000 LeiosNotify requests while a w40 responder throws MkExnLeiosNotifyExcessiveRequests at 100 and drops the connection. Connections are duplex, so our w40a relays act as initiator even on peer-dialled links. A w40 peer would therefore show exactly this shape: connect, run, drop, reconnect.

Several observations fit. The connections are inbound on port 3001 from varying ephemeral ports, so the peer redials rather than holding one link. The reason is BearerClosed, the remote closing mid-protocol rather than a reset. MkExnLeiosNotifyExcessiveRequests appears zero times fleet-wide, which is expected when we are the high-cap side: the exception is raised and logged by the low-cap peer, not by us.

One observation does not fit, and it is the discriminating one. The overrun is triggered by LeiosNotify traffic, so churn should scale with Leios activity. It does not:

19:10–20:05 off 2584 2.2 85 0.03

21:10–22:05 off 2610 2.2 67 0.02

Leios traffic is 10–40× higher in the on-hour while the peer's churn is flat, and marginally lower in the busy hour. A load-triggered pipelining overrun cannot produce that. The steady ~2500/h rate across all conditions looks timer-driven rather than load-driven.

The mechanism is plausible but the discriminating test does not support it. The reconnect cadence below narrows it further.

Reconnect cadence

On a single relay over 30 minutes the peer produced 145 mux errors. Gaps between consecutive failures are extremely regular:

p25 11.4s   median 12.5s   p75 13.5s   p95 14.2s   max 14.4s
142 of 144 gaps fall in the 10-15s bucket; the rest are 5-10s

The hard ceiling just under 15s suggests a timer rather than a load-dependent failure. Two candidates, with what the data says about each:

  • Starlink satellite handovers. These occur on a globally synchronised 15-second schedule, so failures would cluster at fixed offsets in wall-clock time. They do not: binning the failure timestamps modulo 15 seconds gives a near-uniform spread, circular concentration R = 0.036 where 0 is uniform and 1 is perfectly phase-locked. Not supported.
  • A per-connection timeout on the peer's side. A timer that starts when each connection is established produces exactly this shape: tight interval, hard ceiling, no alignment to absolute time. The reason string is BearerClosed, meaning the remote closed the connection, which is consistent. Not confirmed, but it fits what is observed.

Neither the w40 pipelining mechanism nor satellite handovers explain the pattern. A fixed timeout on the peer's side does, and the evidence for which timeout would be in its logs.

The peer stopped abruptly at Sat 11:30 and produced exactly zero events thereafter. Splitting the loaded weekend on that boundary shows fleet egress is slightly higher without it, so the peer does not drive relay bandwidth. See the bandwidth section.

Monitoring cost

Alloy absorbed ~15× the vote log lines without stress: CPU ~0.03 cores, RSS flat at 200–300 MiB.

Mimir series for vote metrics went 577 → 2,078, entirely from leios_logmetrics_leios_votes_cast_total and leios_logmetrics_leios_voted_weight_total, each 15 → 765 series. Both carry a promoted voterId label.

Stale series are exactly what drives cardinality, so 765 is the right number for a monitoring-cost argument and the wrong one for counting keys.

voterId is a per-host index 1..76, not a pool hash, so it is bounded and safe at this scale. It is the same stage-label promotion flagged for removal before a larger test; it was dropped on the acquired side but not on cast or weight.

Methodology

The duty cycle is the critical trap. The transaction generator runs a one-hour on/off cycle and votes only exist while it is on — EBs carry votes only when there are transactions to endorse. Fleet votes drop to exactly zero during every off hour, under load as well as at baseline.

Consequences:

  1. The load test is only live 50% of wall-clock time.
  2. Any before/after comparison must align on-hour to on-hour, i.e. an offset that is an even number of hours. Comparing an on-hour to an off-hour produces garbage.
  3. The load ran continuously from 14:02 on 10-09, giving 29 on-hours through 10-12 00:00.

One baseline hour is not enough. The baseline hours vary enough that a single one can be a 34% outlier on egress, which is sufficient to manufacture a convincing effect out of nothing. Five phase-aligned baselines were used here; that is the minimum that made the spread visible. Any claim from this environment that rests on one baseline window should be treated as unverified.

Known confounders:

  • Every host restarts on a 12-hour staggered timer (RuntimeMaxSec = 12 * 3600), to cap a node memory leak before OOM. Restarts arrive in waves across the weekend. They were tested as an explanation for the unexplained off-hour egress variance and do not explain it: Pearson r = −0.047 between relay restarts and fleet egress across the 28 off-hours, and the three highest off-hours all had zero relay restarts. They remain a confounder in principle, just not a demonstrated one here.
  • The ten leiosred BPs additionally restarted at 14:02 for the key deploy, inflating load1's connection churn. load1 is therefore the least representative of the 29 loaded hours and is the one hour where mux errors sit above the unloaded band.
  • Relay connection counts drift up to 40% between windows, independently of the test. Control BP peer counts do not, being config-pinned.
  • The 10-09 load hours carried less transaction throughput than the baselines (32.7–39.7/s against 42.3–46.4/s). This cuts against the CPU and lateness findings, i.e. those effects are if anything understated.

Data source: Mimir and Loki via the Grafana datasource proxy. Node-exporter network rates come from the instance:node_network_*_bytes_excluding_lo:rate5m recording rules, which are 5-minute averages over a 60-second scrape — exact for means, 15–25× low on peaks. The 1-second figures come from each host's local netdata instead.

Loki stores each cardano-node log line twice, raw plus alloy-decoded. A line-filter query such as {systemd_unit="cardano-node.service"} |= "MuxErrored" matches both copies and must be halved; a query selecting on the indexed kind label matches one copy and must not be. Prefer the indexed form: those streams carry ~11.7M lines/hour across the 9 relays, so a line filter over a multi-hour dashboard range will not complete.

Why not 1-minute instead of 5-minute from Mimir?

The scrape interval is 60s, so a finer rate than the rate5m recording rules is available from the raw counters at no extra cost. It is worth knowing how much that buys, because the answer bounds what Mimir can ever say about bursts.

rate(node_network_transmit_bytes_total[1m]) returns nothing: a 1-minute window holds one sample at a 60s scrape and rate() needs two. The working forms are irate(raw[5m]), rate(raw[2m]) and idelta(raw[2m])/60, which all return the same value — the delta across the last two samples, i.e. a true 1-minute rate.

So moving from 5-minute to 1-minute recovers roughly 1.5× more peak and is free, but it still captures only 10–18% of the real peak against 6–15% before. Both are the wrong instrument for a burst question; the gap between them is small next to the gap to ground truth. This is a property of the 60-second scrape, not of the averaging window — no PromQL expression can recover detail the scrape never sampled.

Practical notes if you use the 1-minute form:

  • Use irate(), not rate(...[1m]).
  • The raw counters are per-device and include loopback, so filter device!="lo" and sum() yourself. The recording rules do both for you.
  • Means and totals are unaffected by window length and are exact either way. Only peaks differ, so there is no reason to change how averages are computed.

Raw data archive

Four netdata archives, all at the hosts' native collection resolution. Sizes are apparent; the directories sit on zfs, where du reports roughly half.

  • data-1s/ — interface bandwidth, 19 hosts, six 1-hour windows on 10-09, 21,600 rows per host, 5.1 MB. The only copy of the two unloaded baseline hours.
  • data-1s-wide/ — CPU, memory, disk, TCP, PSI, per-process and the full network chart family for the same six windows. ~100 charts and ~210 dimensions per host, 77.1M rows, 291 MB. Not uniformly 1-second: PSI is 2-second and system.load is 5-second.
  • data-weekend/ — interface bandwidth, 19 hosts, 58 continuous hours from 10-09 14:00 to 10-12 00:00, 208,801 rows per host, 48 MB.
  • data-weekend-wide/ — the wide chart set over those same 58 hours, 19 hosts, 35.7M–55.7M rows each, 745.6M rows in total, 2.7 GB.

3.0 GB across 76 files. Integrity checks: row counts match expectation exactly (19 × 6 × 3600 = 410,400 and 19 × 208,801), the weekend data has zero gaps and zero duplicate seconds across all 19 hosts, and the four load windows present in both data-1s and data-weekend agree on every one of 14,400 overlapping seconds on each host spot-checked.

Every file passes gzip -t, which is the check that matters: a dropped SSH ControlMaster severs the stream mid-record and leaves a file that is large and plausible but incomplete. One host was caught that way and re-pulled. MANIFEST.txt records apparent size and full sha256 for all 76 files, and data-weekend-wide/MANIFEST.txt additionally records row, chart and dimension counts and the exact time span per host — all 19 spanning 2026-10-09 14:00:00 to 2026-10-12 00:00:00 in three day-chunks, with no warnings.

How long the source stays re-pullable. The hosts run netdata 2.8.4 with no [db] section in /etc/netdata/netdata.conf, so the upstream default applies: tier 0 keeps 1-second samples for a requested 14 days, bounded in practice by disk. Measured on 2026-10-12, actual tier-0 retention ran from 8d22h on leios1-rel-a-1 to 21d8h on leiosred1-bp-a-1, most hosts at or above the 14-day request.

The 10-09 windows therefore stay re-pullable from the hosts until about 2026-10-18 even on the shortest-retention host. The archives matter for reproducibility after that date, not because the source is about to vanish. Mimir never stored anything finer than a 5-minute average, so for sub-minute behaviour these archives remain the only source.

Open questions

Questions 1 and 2 are partly answered in followup-600-seats.md, which compares the two costs across the 2026-10-15 cut from ~900 seats to ~600. Lateness did not move; certification is confounded there by a community load test running across the change, so neither is closed.

  1. Does certification recover when the load stops? The effect clears p < 0.05 but the loaded and unloaded block distributions overlap. A post-load period returning to the 34–42% band would confirm the load is the cause rather than a drift across the week.
  2. Is EB announcement lateness settling? 2.8–6.1% across the weekend with the highest value in the last hour sampled.
  3. What drives off-hour egress? It spans 5.7–69.8 Mbit/s across 28 off-hours with no votes at all and no relation to the external peer. Unexplained, and large enough to swamp any two-window comparison.
  4. What was the external peer? A Starlink-connected address, not a registered pool, that redialled all 9 relays every ~12.5s from Fri 18:30 and stopped abruptly at Sat 11:30. Neither the w40 pipelining mechanism nor satellite handovers fit the flat, non-phase-locked cadence; a fixed per-connection timeout on its side does.
  5. Per-vote wire cost was never measured. The bandwidth result is an aggregate; it yields no per-vote byte figure and should not be extrapolated to mainnet fanout.
  6. Resource headroom on the real BPs. The three control BPs run 2 cores and 3.7 GB with under 0.9 GB available and CPU pressure of 6–12%, an order of magnitude more contended than db-sync. Not misbehaving; worth a look.
